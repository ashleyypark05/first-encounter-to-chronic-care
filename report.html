From First Encounter to Chronic Care

A Multi-Level Analysis of Patient Utilization, Access Gaps, and Social Determinants in a Regional Health System

Using Stormont Vail Health encounter records linked to patient demographics, provider geography, and social determinants, this analysis investigates what drives whether a patient who first visits the healthcare system ever returns. Three analytical threads are woven together: patient utilization patterns (who returns, by age and diagnosis); geospatial access gap mapping (where providers are sparse relative to demand); and social determinant profiling (which structural barriers cluster in underserved areas). Core findings show that age and diagnosis type are highly predictive of return (χ² p < 0.001), that 24 geographic clusters qualify as access gaps under HPSA standards, and that social burden is concentrated in the same areas where patients are least likely to continue care.

1. Introduction & Research Questions

Longitudinal care — patients who return to the same health system after an initial encounter — is a foundational indicator of continuity and health system effectiveness. For a regional system like Stormont Vail Health (SVH) serving both urban Shawnee County and rural central Kansas, understanding who returns and who doesn't is not only a quality metric but a resource allocation question.

This analysis treats the "first encounter" as a decision point: given a patient's age, diagnosis category, and geographic location, what is the probability they become a longitudinal patient? It then maps where geographic provider gaps intersect with low return rates and high social burden, and offers concrete pathway recommendations.

"For patients in the SVH system, what structural, demographic, and geographic factors predict whether a first clinical encounter leads to sustained longitudinal care?"

  • Utilization — Do age group and primary diagnosis type significantly predict multi-visit status?
  • Geography — Which demand zones in the region lack sufficient provider coverage by HPSA standards?
  • SDOH — Do social determinant burdens (transport, housing) cluster in access-gap areas, and does this explain low return rates?

2. Data & Pipeline

Six tables were used from the DataFest dataset: encounters, patients, diagnosis, providers, social determinants, and TIGER census block group centroids. Analysis was conducted in Python (pandas, scipy, sklearn, folium) and R (overlakepark spatial pipeline).

3. Patient Utilization Patterns

Shawnee County comprises approximately 48.9% of all multiple-visit encounters in the SVH system, making it the dominant proxy for longitudinal care behavior. The analysis centers on this county while accounting for the broader regional picture.

Age as a Structural Predictor

Older patients return at far higher rates regardless of diagnosis category. Patients aged 40–64 and 65+ consistently dominate multi-visit counts. Young adults (21–39) represent only 12% of the multi-visit share despite having symptom-based return rates of 83.9% — identifying them as the highest-upside conversion opportunity.

Key Finding — Age × Diagnosis The 65+ cohort with musculoskeletal (M) or circulatory (I) diagnoses returns at 94–96%, effectively demonstrating built-in longitudinal behavior. Children's return rate on routine (Z) codes at 88.5% shows that routine pediatric care is already longitudinal by design. The gap worth closing is early adulthood — high return potential, low follow-through.

Diagnosis Category Distributions

Single-visit patients are disproportionately concentrated in routine (Z) and symptom (R) codes — consistent with acute, resolved, or one-time encounters. Multi-visit patients show stronger representation across chronic-management chapters: E (endocrine/diabetes), M (musculoskeletal), I (circulatory), and K (digestive), all conditions requiring sustained management.

Conversion Opportunity Young adults (21–39) presenting with R-codes return at 83.9% when they do return — but represent only 12% of the multi-visit share. Post-visit follow-up protocols targeting this cohort could meaningfully increase longitudinal engagement, particularly for patients whose symptom presentations suggest underlying conditions that will recur.

4. Geospatial Access Gap Analysis

Provider addresses were geocoded and assigned to DBSCAN-derived demand clusters. Clusters were scored using the Health Professional Shortage Area (HPSA) standard: clusters with a population-to-provider ratio exceeding 3,500:1 were flagged as access gaps. Six clusters had zero providers entirely.

5. Hypothesis Testing

Three chi-square tests were conducted on the patient-level conversion dataset (first observed encounter mapped to total encounter count). All tests used Shawnee County patients with non-missing age group, diagnosis chapter, and admission type.

All six hypotheses confirmed Age group, diagnosis type, and admission type are each independently and significantly associated with whether a patient returns for additional care. The relationship holds even within narrow subgroups: young adults with symptom codes return at higher rates than those with routine codes, confirming that diagnosis content still matters within demographic segments.

Fragility Compounding Patients in gap areas who also carry SDOH flags are more than twice as likely to be single-visit chronic patients as those in adequately-served areas without SDOH burden. Geographic access deficits and social risk are not independent — they cluster on the same patients. Screening coverage in gap areas is only 14.3%, meaning the true burden is likely substantially underestimated.

MyChart Engagement as a Proxy

Lower MyChart activation status was associated with higher average social burden scores among screened patients. Since MyChart engagement proxies for how connected a patient is to the SVH ecosystem, this suggests that patients most at risk of discontinuing care are also the hardest to reach through digital channels alone.

7. Recommendations

Sustaining Longitudinal Care

Convert high-return demographics

Deepen continuity with the 40–64 and 65+ cohorts presenting with M, I, and E diagnoses. Already at 92–96% return rates; the goal is preventing attrition at the margins through proactive follow-up scheduling.

Engage young adult single-visit patients

Young adults (21–39) return at 83.9% when presenting with symptoms. Automated post-visit follow-up protocols — particularly for R-code first encounters — represent the highest-upside conversion opportunity.

Use routine care as a retention anchor

Z-code visits carry high return rates across all age groups. Annual wellness visits position themselves naturally as longitudinal touchpoints. Scheduling the next appointment before discharge from a Z visit costs nothing and measurably improves continuity.

SDOH-informed discharge planning

For patients flagged with transport or housing barriers, offer telehealth as the default follow-up modality. Schedule the next appointment before discharge. A single care navigator role could address the 172-day average inter-visit gap in gap areas.

Addressing Access Gaps

Minimum — Mobile & Telehealth

Deploy mobile or satellite clinic units to the top 5 gap clusters (3, 14, 13, 24, 4). Establish a telehealth referral network specifically for E and M chapter patients in the 6 zero-provider clusters. Priority: E and M chronic management patients who are currently single-visit.

Optimal — Provider Recruitment

Rural incentive programs targeting 1–2 PCPs per critical gap cluster. Co-locate chronic care and pharmacy services given the high E/M burden in gap areas. Provider recruitment is a multi-year initiative; mobile coverage is the bridge.

Care Navigator Role Gap-area patients average 172 days between visits — nearly six months. A dedicated care navigator embedded in the gap-cluster outreach program could coordinate scheduling, address SDOH barriers proactively, and reduce the 39.8% single-visit chronic rate that currently characterizes these populations.

Expand SDOH Screening

Only 14.3% of gap-area patients are currently screened for social determinants. Expanding screening to hospital admissions and outpatient encounters in gap clusters would surface the estimated ~18.8% of patients carrying transport or housing barriers, enabling targeted intervention before a patient disappears from the system after a first encounter.

8. Limitations

SDOH selection bias. Social determinant data covers only ~6.8% of patients, with screening concentrated in Shawnee County hospital admissions. Prevalence estimates cannot be generalized to the full patient population or to rural areas. SDOH flags are patient-level risk signals, not community-level prevalence measurements.

Geocoding coverage. Provider address geocoding was conducted via Census Geocoder batch API with some Non_Exact matches retained. Rural provider locations and recently opened practices may be under-represented.

Single-visit definition. A patient coded as single-visit in this dataset may have sought care elsewhere, or may have had additional encounters not captured in SVH records. "Single-visit" reflects SVH system engagement, not total healthcare utilization.

FIPS precision. Block groups with fewer than 11 patients are coded as *Unspecified per the data documentation, which systematically under-represents rural and sparse-population areas — precisely the areas most likely to contain access gaps.

Temporal snapshot. The dataset represents a static time window. Seasonal patterns, pandemic-era care disruptions, or recent provider recruitment changes are not captured.
